Closing the Strait of Malacca is a supply problem before it is a navy story. China can be cut off from the Gulf only as far as its oil still comes through a southern gate, and only while stocks, Pacific deliveries, and pipelines fail to fill the gap. Beijing has spent twenty quiet years and a great deal of money still trying to shrink that gap.

The strait is a funnel between Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula, narrowing to a few kilometers of usable lane near Singapore. A large share of the oil and dry cargo moving between the Indian Ocean and the Pacific passes that funnel. The classic Chinese statement of the problem, the Malacca dilemma, treated roughly four-fifths of China's oil imports as hostages of this single passage. Pipelines have since lowered the fraction. They have not retired it. Middle Eastern and African crude still reaches Chinese terminals by riding the southern approaches.

John Mearsheimer reads a blockade as the tool of a sea power. The United States does not have to occupy China's interior to hurt Chinese power. It has to threaten the ships that feed Chinese factories. Under anarchy, nobody is in charge, so intentions stay unknown, so Beijing plans for what America can do, not for a promise that it never will. Energy dependence is an advantage for whoever holds the outer sea. Security is one-sided at a narrow strait, because one navy's open lane is the other navy's closed valve. Relative power is getting to decide which of those it is.

Jeffrey Sachs hears a different story in the same ships. A blockade of energy and food trade is what follows when one country runs out of rules and reaches for force. It confirms, inside the targeted country, that the American system is not a safe home. The Military-Industrial Complex can keep the posture going, because a crisis at a narrow strait is a fleet job with a budget line. The act meant to punish China is also the act that makes an energy partnership across Eurasia look like common sense.

Professor Jiang asks for the arithmetic before the coalition. Set Chinese crude imports at eleven million barrels a day. Split the flow into three pipes. Six million barrels a day still depend on a southern strait. The other five million arrive from the Pacific side or by land, and a Malacca-only stop does not touch them. Put a hypothetical stock of eight hundred million barrels behind the system, and call it a scenario dial rather than a published reserve. If every import stopped, the dial would read on the order of seventy days. If only the southern six million stopped, the deficit is six million a day and the same dial reads on the order of four months. Rationing starts before the tanks are empty. The dilemma literature divides stocks by total imports and therefore overstates the speed of a narrow closure.

The composition of the surviving five million is the part of the map that matters. Design capacity on the three inland crude lines is public and limited. The Myanmar line from the Bay of Bengal toward Yunnan is about twenty-two million tons a year. The Kazakhstan line is about twenty million. The spur from Russia's eastern pipeline system into Daqing is about thirty million. Together that is a little under one and a half million barrels a day. The Power of Siberia line, at a design near thirty-eight billion cubic meters a year, displaces some seaborne liquefied gas and leaves the crude gap intact. Russian crude loaded on the Pacific, at Kozmino, never enters the Indian Ocean, and cargoes from the Americas can cross the Pacific directly. Including the Pacific turns the siege into a larger war.

Rerouting is the first counter. Cargoes that cannot use Malacca can try the Sunda Strait, the Lombok Strait, or the eastern Indonesian exits. The journey is longer. Freight and insurance rise. Some of this diversion is already normal, because the largest tankers prefer deeper water. A closure that is real in economic terms has to price every southern gate, not only the famous one. Each added gate adds coastline, adds a third country's territorial sea, and adds a chance that the blockade stops being a bilateral squeeze. Mearsheimer would count that chance as the mechanism by which chokepoint wars widen. The offshore power wants a valve. The geography offers an archipelago.

The second answer is to use less. Diesel for agriculture and for the armed forces is protected. Private road fuel is not. Export-oriented petrochemicals are cut before household heating. Refineries are told to favor the barrel used at home over the product sold abroad. A modern industrial country can take a partial loss of crude for a season if its grids still have coal, nuclear, hydro, and pipeline gas, and if managers will idle the least necessary factory. The industries that halt first are the ones whose exports earn the foreign money China uses to buy the next cargo. An energy squeeze and a dollar squeeze work together. Tankers inside the blue insurance system stop. Cargoes that still move are the ones paid in yuan, gold, or a two-country balance, under insurance written outside American law.

The third counter is the overland and Arctic option set. Pipelines at full design replace a slice of the southern deficit, not the deficit itself. Pushing them harder in a crisis depends on Russian surplus production, on Kazakh politics, and on the security of a Burmese corridor that has its own internal wars. The Northern Sea Route along the Russian Arctic is a summer and shoulder-season lane, shortened on the map between northern energy projects and Chinese ports, and constrained by ice class, icebreakers, insurance, and depth of traffic. Its annual volume is a rounding error beside the oil that normally rides Malacca. It is a relief valve, not a replacement artery. Gwadar and the Pakistani corridor are a political option with berth limits and an insurgency problem.

Run the gambit forward one quarter. Month one: ships already east of the gates discharge, and stocks draw down on a published schedule. The external price of tanker insurance spikes. Month two: southern crude is partly rerouted, partly replaced by Pacific Russian barrels, and partly lost. Pipeline utilization is forced upward. Export factories on the south coast take the first enforced holidays. Month three: if the Pacific gate is still open, the system is injured and functioning. If the Pacific gate has been closed as well, stocks approach the level where military and agricultural priorities crowd out the rest, and the industrial core contracts. This is the calculation behind China's peacetime overpayment for redundant routes. The option value equals the probability of a siege, multiplied by the output destroyed per month of shortage. These routes look overpriced until a siege probability is included in the price.

Mearsheimer's warning sits on the third month. A continental power that watches its stocks fall does not experience the blockade as a limited navy measure. It experiences it as an attempt on the political survival of the state. The response set includes economic payback, pressure on other countries, and the risk of attacks on the blockading fleet. Such moves rest on one idea: states rank survival above the other side's rules for a fight. Washington's planners know this, which is why a real blockade is tied to a Taiwan crisis rather than switched on as a punishment by itself. The energy valve is an add-on to a larger fight, and add-ons are how larger fights get away from the people who started them.

Sachs would count a different loss. Allies who depend on the same strait for their own Gulf oil are asked to bless a closure that raises their prices and their shipping risk. Gulf producers are asked to give up Chinese demand, or to accept America policing their customers. The unipolar habit assumes those asks will be obeyed. A multipolar energy market gives the producers a second buyer and, after recent freezes of reserves, a reason to keep that buyer. Coercion at the strait speeds up the shift from the first episode: the next barrel billed outside the dollar, because the dollar system has shown it can stop the ship.

The plain result is two numbers and two maps. China can endure a Malacca-only closure for months, with damage, by drawing stocks, sending ships the long way, pushing pipelines to about one and a half million barrels a day, and leaning on Pacific crude. China cannot endure a full southern-and-Pacific energy siege for long without factories shutting down hard, and that fuller siege is a regional war, not just a strait. Beijing's gambit is to make the first closure disappointing and the second enormous. Washington's gambit is to threaten both, and hope the threat changes Chinese behavior before either closure is tried. Under unknowable intentions, meaning neither side can be sure of the other, both sides prepare for the test anyway.
