The trap isn’t a debt. It’s a simple rule about how oil gets paid for, and it connects two countries that would rather depend less on each other. China finances the American government because the oil that feeds Chinese factories is billed in dollars, and the only safe pile large enough to store those dollars is United States Treasury debt. This is the connection that matters. Beijing's export earnings become Washington's room to borrow and spend. Follow the money from the factory, through the oil bill, to the government bond. The mirror holds only while the next barrel must be bought in dollars. When that rule fails, the loop doesn’t bend. It breaks.

In nineteen seventy-one the United States stopped promising gold for dollars. The dollar no longer promised metal. It promised something else: that the world would keep needing it. But that need had to be built. The deal with Saudi Arabia supplied it. Oil would be priced in dollars, so the money left over would need somewhere to go. Surpluses would be parked in dollar assets. Every importer, whatever its flag, would need dollars to buy energy. John Mearsheimer reads this as a great power locking in its lead. The leading state doesn’t trust promises. It ties each partner's survival to its currency, so the lead grows. A rival currency that can buy oil claims the same oil. One side's safety is the other side's risk.

Jeffrey Sachs reads the same setup as a privilege the system later mistook for a law of nature. The financial center and the arms industry came to depend on the same flow of money. Deficits could run for years because foreigners had to hold the paper. The mistake was to treat an advantage built at a particular moment in history as a permanent right. Then the system that carried the money became a way to put pressure on other countries.

China entered the loop through trade, not through an alliance. Joining the World Trade Organization opened demand in rich countries to Chinese goods. The surplus arrived in dollars, while more of the fuel keeping the factories running came by sea, from producers who also billed in dollars. Professor Jiang treats the move as forced. At each round, Beijing could park dollars in Treasuries, or try to swap them and find the next oil cargo still for sale only in dollars. Recycling was the best move on the board. The Treasury market was deep enough to take the surplus without pushing the price against the seller. The arrangement held because China, alone, couldn’t change what the world paid for energy.

Call the Treasury pile what it really is. It’s savings that someone else can block, even when the owner needs the money. The money stays usable only while the country holding it chooses to leave it alone. Mearsheimer assumes that restraint can’t be known ahead of time, because intentions stay hidden. A careful state therefore allows for a freeze, a seizure, or a political condition, even when Washington's public language sounds reassuring. For years that chance looked small. China's Treasuries helped pay for the American household that bought Chinese goods. Each side needed the other, and that need passed for trust.

The chance moved when the dollar system froze the savings of a nuclear-armed oil seller. What mattered was the lesson other countries drew. If government bonds held in American banks could be switched off over a weekend, then Treasuries had looked safer than they were for any country that might face Washington. Sachs places that choice in a habit of punishment from the top. Sanctions had replaced a negotiated deal, and the cost came later, as other countries began to trust the system less. Surplus countries had treated dollar savings as normal savings, and then changed their minds. The change doesn’t reverse when a spokesman calls the case unique. What people believe about the custodian changes when they see what it actually does.

Mearsheimer draws a plain conclusion, with no sermon attached. China can’t stay the workshop of a system whose banker can switch off China's savings. Staying free to act means holding less national wealth in a form a rival can cancel. Real power slips if a country has to ask a rival for permission to use its own savings. The answer is a series of small decisions about where the next payment goes. Fewer fresh purchases of Treasuries, more gold, and more state-to-state oil contracts. More swap lines between central banks, and more attempts to settle trade through payment systems that don’t end in New York.

Jiang names what keeps the system together. The grip isn’t the printing press. The grip is the web of partner banks, the message system that carries payment orders, and the law that can tell those banks to stop. An adversary breaks that grip where a real good changes hands. Energy is the right place to look, because the bill comes back with every cargo. Currency pegs reinforce that dependence: they tie the producer's own money to the dollar. A producer tied to the dollar prefers to be paid in dollars. A producer that can be paid in money it spends on Chinese machines, or in gold, or in a mixed basket, has a second way to get paid. Security patrons, arms suppliers, and long-term buyers change the political price of using that second way.

This is why the break is narrower than a collapse of the dollar. The loop that traps China doesn’t need every barrel on earth priced in dollars. It needs China's next barrel priced in dollars. The pressure applies only to the next purchase, not to the oil already bought. The pile of Treasuries already owned can sit quietly for a full decade. New buying stops when export earnings no longer have to be turned into the currency of the next cargo. A Gulf producer, with enough output to cover the growth in China's imports by sea, bills a standing volume of its crude in yuan and clears that payment on a rail that never enters American hands. The volume need not be most of that producer's sales to the world. It must be most of the extra barrels China would otherwise have bought with new dollars.

Then the math changes direction. China's goods surplus can continue, and dollars can still arrive from buyers in the United States and in allied markets. The forced bid for Treasuries, the bid that existed only because the oil seller would take nothing else, is gone. What remains is a choice about where to keep the money, and that choice feels the hostage problem. Washington's sensible reply, in this model, is to remind Beijing that the Treasuries it already owns can still be frozen. The reminder is meant to scare others away from leaving. If plans stay hidden, the reminder points the wrong way. It raises the estimated risk of confiscation, which gives Beijing more reason to reduce its exposure before a crisis begins. Sachs would call this the center writing its own exit. Each show of force gives countries with export surpluses another reason to find an alternative payment system. It’s insurance against seizure.

The arms industry arrives as another claim on the same loop. A dollar that foreign central banks must buy is a dollar that helps fund the security services of the leading state. If official foreign demand thins out, the interest needed to sell new Treasury debt has to come from home savers and allied private money. That shift is a fact about power. Higher borrowing costs tax the country that uses financial sanctions as a tool. Jiang closes on one plain point. The weapon and the funding base depend on the same system, so using the weapon gradually wears down the funding base. Depreciation stays slow while China's next barrel forces the recycling. It speeds up after the oil bill moves to another currency, because that bill was what kept the recycling going.

Nothing here requires Beijing to win a popularity contest for its currency. A spare way to pay can be messy, expensive, and still enough. Local money, gold as a neutral bridge, and a basket instead of the dollar are enough if they cover energy and some machinery. The United States remains the deepest market for savings. Allies remain inside the blue system because their protection is written there, and because depending on it limits what they can plan. The break isn’t the whole world walking out. The break is the exit of the one surplus economy whose oil bill made the mirror necessary.

Mearsheimer adds the last limit. The leading state won’t accept a gentle loss of its money weapon if it can make leaving expensive. Tariffs, technology denial, and the threat of being cut off from finance are attempts to restore the old payoffs. They’re also new information, telling the country that the leading state's intentions are hostile enough to justify moving faster. The two sides can therefore jump from a slow shift to a fast shift without either side wanting a crisis. That’s how the trap ends. It isn’t a crash of the dollar against every good. It’s a refusal, at the edge, to park China's surplus in claims on the American state. The mirror doesn’t shatter everywhere. It shatters along the single path that ran from the factory, to the barrel, to the bond.
