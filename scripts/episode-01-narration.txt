The trap is not a debt. It is a pricing convention. China finances the American state because the barrel of oil that feeds Chinese factories is invoiced in dollars, and the only asset large enough to store those dollars is the debt of the United States Treasury. This is the hegemon's mirror. Beijing's industrial surplus appears, on the far side of the glass, as Washington's fiscal capacity. The mirror holds only while the next barrel must be bought with dollars. When that condition fails, the recycling loop does not bend. It breaks.

In nineteen seventy-one the United States closed the gold window. The dollar no longer promised metal. It promised demand, and demand had to be manufactured. The arrangements of the mid-nineteen-seventies, built around Saudi Arabia, supplied it. Oil would be priced in dollars. Surpluses would be placed in dollar assets. Every importer, regardless of flag, would need dollars to buy energy. John Mearsheimer's offensive realism reads the design as power maximization under anarchy. The leading state does not trust intentions. It binds each client's survival to its currency so that relative power compounds. A rival currency that can buy oil is a rival claim on the same physical surplus. Security, in that sense, is zero-sum.

Jeffrey Sachs reads the same architecture as the founding privilege of a system that later mistook itself for a natural order. The financial hub and the Military-Industrial Complex became one circuit. Deficits could be run because foreigners had to hold the paper. The hub's error, on this account, was to treat a historical convenience as a permanent right, and then to weaponize the plumbing that made the convenience possible.

China entered the loop through trade, not through alliance. Accession to the World Trade Organization opened rich-country demand to Chinese manufactures. The surplus arrived in dollars. A rising share of the energy that kept the factories running arrived by sea, from producers who invoiced in dollars. Professor Jiang's game treats the resulting strategy as forced. At each round, Beijing could park dollars in Treasuries, or it could try to convert them and discover that the next oil cargo was still for sale only in dollars. Recycling was the dominant strategy. The Treasury market was deep enough to absorb the surplus without moving the price against the seller. The equilibrium was stable because China, acting alone, could not reprice the world's energy.

Call the accumulated Treasuries what the game requires them to be. They are reserves, and they are a hostage. A hostage is an asset whose value depends on the custodian's restraint. Offensive realism assumes that restraint cannot be known in advance. Intentions are private information. A rational state therefore assigns a probability to a freeze, a diversion, or a political condition, and it assigns that probability even when the custodian's public language is mild. For years the probability looked small. China's Treasuries helped finance the American household that bought Chinese goods. Mutual vulnerability imitated trust.

The probability moved when the dollar system immobilized the reserves of a nuclear-armed commodity exporter. What mattered was the information released. If sovereign paper held inside American clearing could be made unusable in a weekend, then the liquidity premium on Treasuries had been mispriced for every state that might one day face Washington. Sachs places the decision in the habits of unipolar enforcement. Sanctions had become a substitute for a negotiated settlement. The cost was delayed. It was paid in credibility. Surplus states that had treated dollar assets as apolitical savings revised their beliefs. The revision does not reverse when a spokesman says the case was unique. Beliefs about a custodian are updated by deeds.

Mearsheimer's inference follows without a moral premise. China cannot remain the workshop of a system whose clearing agent can disarm China's reserves. Survival, defined as freedom of action under anarchy, requires cutting the share of national wealth held in a form a rival can nullify. Relative power falls if a state must ask permission to mobilize its own savings. The answer is a change at the margin. Fewer fresh purchases of Treasuries. More gold. More state-to-state oil contracts. More swap lines between central banks. More attempts to clear trade on rails that do not terminate in New York.

Jiang specifies the weapon's grip. The grip is not the printing press. The grip is the mesh of correspondent banks, the messaging system that carries payment instructions, and the legal reach that can tell those banks to stop. An adversary breaks that grip at the contract where a physical good changes hands. Energy is the right contract because energy is the largest repetitive invoice in world trade. Currency pegs are the supporting machinery. A producer pegged to the dollar prefers dollar receipts. A producer that can be paid in a currency it spends on Chinese capital goods, or in gold, or in a basket, has acquired a second option. Security patrons, arms suppliers, and long-term buyers change the political price of using that option.

This is why the shatter point is narrower than a collapse of the dollar. The loop that traps China does not require every barrel on earth to be priced in dollars. It requires China's next barrel to be priced in dollars. Recycling is a marginal compulsion. The stock of Treasuries already owned can sit for a decade. The flow that adds to the stock stops when export earnings no longer have to be turned into the currency of the next cargo. A Gulf producer, with output large enough to cover the growth in Chinese seaborne imports, invoices a structural volume of crude in yuan and clears the payment on a rail that never enters a United States custodian. The volume need not be most of that producer's sales to the world. It must be most of the incremental barrels China would otherwise have bought with new dollars.

Then the arithmetic changes its sign. China's goods surplus can continue. Dollars can still arrive from buyers in the United States and in allied markets. The obligatory bid for Treasuries, the bid that existed only because the oil seller would take nothing else, is gone. What remains is a portfolio choice, and a portfolio choice is sensitive to the hostage problem. Under offensive realism, Washington's rational reply to a yuan oil contract is a reminder that the stock already held can still be frozen. The reminder is designed to deter exit. In a game of unknowable intentions, the reminder carries the opposite sign. It raises the estimated chance of confiscation, and therefore raises the return to reducing the stock before a crisis rather than during one. Sachs would describe the sequence as the hegemon drafting its own departure. Each coercive display teaches surplus states that a second settlement system is not a taste. It is insurance against seizure.

The Military-Industrial Complex enters the endgame as a claimant on the same loop. A dollar that foreign central banks must buy is a dollar that helps fund the security apparatus of the leading state. If official foreign demand thins, the yield required to clear Treasury auctions has to be found among domestic savers and allied private capital. That shift is a relative-power fact. Higher sustained funding costs tax the state that uses financial sanctions as an instrument. Jiang's model closes on that identity. The weapon and the funding base are one object. Use of the weapon depreciates the funding base. Depreciation stays slow while the marginal barrel enforces recycling. It speeds up after the barrel moves, because the barrel was the hidden mandate.

Nothing in this break requires Beijing to win a contest of monetary prestige. A multipolar clearing system can be fragmented, expensive, and still sufficient. Local-currency settlement, gold as a neutral bridge, and narrow pegs that track a basket rather than the dollar are enough if they cover energy and a slice of industrial equipment. The United States remains the deepest capital market. Allies remain inside the blue system because their security is underwritten there, and because dependence constrains their intentions. The break is not a universal walkout. The break is the exit of the one surplus economy whose oil bill made the mirror necessary.

Mearsheimer adds the last constraint. The leading state will not accept a smooth decline in the usefulness of its monetary weapon if it can raise the cost of leaving. Tariffs, technology denial, and the threat of financial isolation are rational attempts to restore the old payoffs. They are also new information. They tell the exiting state that intentions are adverse enough to justify speed. The interaction can therefore jump from slow diversification to fast diversification without any player preferring a crisis. That is the structure of the trap's end. It is not a crash of the dollar against all goods. It is a refusal, at the margin, to store China's surplus in claims on the American state. The mirror does not shatter everywhere. It shatters along the single path that ran from the factory, to the barrel, to the bond.
