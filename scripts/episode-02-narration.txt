Ukraine is a width. Two security systems share a plain with no mountain between them, and the width of the land that belongs to neither system is the only shock absorber they have. Treat the state as a buffer, and the war becomes a fight over how thick that absorber must be. Treat the buffer as something both sides must trust, and the fight has only one stable end. The territory splits, because trust is the input the system cannot supply.

The European plain stores distance in people and fields. John Mearsheimer's offensive realism starts from anarchy. No world authority can enforce a promise. Intentions are private, and they change. What can be counted are capabilities: bases, integrated air defense, and long-range fires. Russia, as a great power, counts those things on the Ukrainian steppe and treats them as a future threat even when the stated purpose is defensive. The Western alliance counts Russian capabilities the same way. Security is zero-sum here because one kilometer cannot be both a forward position for Moscow and a forward position for the alliance. Relative power is the distance between the other side's forces and your own interior.

The institutional record shows how the buffer thinned. After the Cold War the alliance expanded across the former Warsaw Pact. In two thousand eight, at Bucharest, it declared that Ukraine and Georgia would become members. It set no date. It created an expectation in one capital and a deadline in another. The government in Kyiv that had balanced between markets was overturned in twenty fourteen. Crimea was seized. The Donbas war began. The Minsk arrangements tried to freeze that war inside a single state. They were not carried out. Minsk left autonomy in the east and control of the border unresolved. Each unimplemented clause taught both sides that a written restraint was a pause in rearmament. By twenty twenty-two the buffer was no longer thick enough, in Russian threat arithmetic, to be left to time.

Jeffrey Sachs' ledger holds a different blueprint, and it is specific. A sovereign Ukraine, economically open to the European Union, militarily closed to both blocs. No foreign bases. No alliance membership. A long treaty with verification, so inspection replaces trust. The reference case is Finland after nineteen forty-five: a state that stayed independent by refusing to become a platform. On this account, the unipolar period is what killed the blueprint. Washington and allied institutions treated neutrality as a waiting room on the way to enlargement. The Military-Industrial Complex had a standing preference for forward infrastructure over a wide neutral zone. Sachs claims that the institutions could not rank a Russian security concern as equal to an enlargement pledge, and that the refusal produced the war they later described as unprovoked.

Professor Jiang turns the disagreement into a game with one hard object. Military alignment is nearly indivisible. Bases, fused intelligence, and binding commitments do not come in smooth doses. There is no dose of NATO that satisfies a Russian demand for depth and a Ukrainian demand for a patron. The neutral center was the bargain that required all three players to prefer a second-best outcome at the same time. Bucharest, the failure of Minsk, and the invasion each removed one player from that bargain. Once the center is empty, the payoffs favor partitions. A partition assigns the western body of the country to Europe's economic and military orbit and assigns the eastern approaches to Russian depth, with a line both sides can fortify. The line is the geometry that remains when the indivisible object cannot be shared.

The model prints two borders, and it ranks them by cost. The default equilibrium is a freeze along the fortified line of contact in the east and the south. Crimea stays on the Russian side of the ledger because Sevastopol is the Black Sea Fleet's harbor, and a hostile coast there would put the fleet under an opposing shore. The land connection to that harbor, and the Donbas belt in front of it, function in Russian planning as depth. West of the freeze, the remaining state keeps Kyiv, the central river cities, and the Polish frontier. It integrates with European markets and continues to receive weapons. Full membership in the alliance stays blocked, because membership would obligate the alliance to treat a renewal of the war as its own war. The great power's red line is observed by amputating the contested east from the alignment question, at the price of a smaller Ukraine.

The second border is the river. The Dnieper is the strongest natural obstacle on this plain. A line there would give Russia depth from the eastern oblasts to a defensible bank, and it would give a western state a shorter frontier. The model does not select this line as the default. Reaching it requires a second, larger war after a land connection to Crimea already exists, and the marginal kilometer west of the present fighting buys less security than it costs in force and in the risk of a wider European war. Move the border only while the next kilometer's security value exceeds its cost. The curves cross, in the baseline case, on the contact line. They cross on the river only if Moscow decides that any Western-armed force east of the water can still threaten the isthmus and the fleet, and if it is willing to pay for that judgment.

Either equilibrium has to solve the capital. A line that twitches through a capital is not a border. It is a permanent crisis. The contact-line state leaves the capital in the western polity, far enough from the freeze to be administered, close enough to the loss to keep the loss politically alive. That political life is why the western polity will not sign a cheerful recognition. Mearsheimer expects de facto partition with de jure protest. Sachs still argues that a neutral whole state would have cost less than either line. Both can accept the same observation. Once blood has repriced the bargain, neither side will trade at the prewar rate. The institutional blueprint remains legible. It is no longer the equilibrium.

The freeze survives only as a repeated game. Each side can cheat. The punishment for cheating is a return to war, which is expensive, so the line holds when the short-run gain from a surprise offensive is smaller than the long-run cost of restarting it. Intentions cannot be inspected, so the game cannot rest on promises. It rests on constraints that operate even if the other side is hostile: a demilitarized band, limits on the long-range systems parked against the line, and monitors whose budgets do not grow if the line moves. Rebuilding armies so that a seasonal offensive looks cheap will dissolve the equilibrium. The partition is final only while cheating stays expensive.

Finance locks the two halves to different patrons, which is Jiang's hidden mechanism. The western state's reconstruction is denominated in euros and dollars, supervised by European institutions and the International Monetary Fund, and conditioned on a security policy those creditors can live with. Conditionality is alignment by another name. The eastern zone's fuel, pensions, and trade clear through Russian fiscal and payment systems. Over time the two zones become incompatible as economic units even if a diplomat still shows one map. Grain moving out of the Black Sea is the side payment that keeps the game quiet. A corridor that lets agriculture earn foreign currency gives both sides a cash reason to prefer the freeze to the gun. Close the corridor, and the side payment disappears. The game returns to pure security.

Sachs would still call this a failure of institutions rather than a law of nature. A neutral state with a shared inspection regime was a multipolar design. Partition is what unipolar habits produce when they meet a great power that will spend heavily to stop a hostile military system on its border. The Military-Industrial Complex only has to keep supplying the forward posture that made neutrality look like surrender. Mearsheimer's reply is that a design is available only if the most powerful players are willing to forgo the alignment. They were not. The buffer was then filled with weapons, and weapons are facts.

The map that results is austere. Blue alliance territory stands on the Polish, Slovak, Hungarian, and Romanian frontiers, a wall rather than a buffer. Amber and gray mark the truncated Ukrainian state, armed from outside, financed from the west, and formally unreconciled. A darker band marks the eastern and southern territories under Russian force, including the fleet harbor, integrated in practice into a different payment and logistics system. Between them sits a thin monitored strip, the last remaining width. The Dnieper stays in the background of the graphic as the escalation line, drawn dashed because the baseline model does not pay for it. The caption says the result is stable under anarchy, costly under every institutional alternative, and priced by a game in which the center could not be shared.
