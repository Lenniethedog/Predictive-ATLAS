Ukraine is a strip of land. Two armed camps share a flat plain, and the land that belongs to neither is the only cushion between them. Treat the state as that cushion, and the war is a fight over how thick the cushion must be. Ask both sides to trust the cushion, and the fight has only one ending that holds. The territory splits on the ground, because trust is still the one thing this setup cannot supply.

The European plain stores distance in towns and fields. John Mearsheimer starts from a simple claim. No world authority can make a promise stick. Intentions are hidden, and they change. What can be counted are weapons: bases, air defense, and long-range missiles. Russia, as a great power, counts those things on the open land of Ukraine and treats them as a future threat even when the stated purpose is defense. The Western alliance counts Russian weapons the same way. Security is one-sided here, because one kilometer cannot be Moscow's forward post and the alliance's at once. Relative power is how far the other side's forces sit from your own home ground.

The record shows how that cushion got thin. After the Cold War the alliance moved east across the old Warsaw Pact. In two thousand eight, at Bucharest, it said Ukraine and Georgia would become members. It set no date. It created a hope in one capital and a deadline in another. The government in Kyiv that had tried to balance between markets was overturned in twenty fourteen. Crimea was taken. The Donbas war began. The Minsk deals tried to freeze that war inside one country. They were not carried out. Minsk left self-rule in the east, and control of the border, unresolved. Each unimplemented promise taught both sides that a written limit was only a pause while they rearmed. By twenty twenty-two the cushion was, in Russia's count of the threat, no longer thick enough to leave to time.

Jeffrey Sachs keeps a different plan, and it is specific. A sovereign Ukraine, open to European trade, closed to both military blocs. No foreign bases. No alliance membership. A long treaty with inspectors, so checking replaces trust. The reference case is Finland after nineteen forty-five: a country that stayed independent by refusing to host someone else's army. On this account, the years when one country ran the system are what killed the plan. Washington and the allied institutions treated staying neutral as a waiting room on the way into the alliance. The Military-Industrial Complex preferred bases near the border over a wide neutral zone. Sachs claims the institutions could not treat a Russian security worry as equal to a promise of enlargement, and that the refusal produced the war they later called unprovoked.

Professor Jiang turns the argument into a game with one hard object. Military alignment is almost impossible to split. Bases, fused intelligence, and binding promises do not come in gentle doses. There is no amount of the alliance that gives Russia the depth it wants and Ukraine the protector it wants. The neutral center was the deal that needed all three players to accept a second-best result at the same time. Bucharest, the failure of Minsk, and the invasion each took one player out of that deal. Once the center is empty, the payoffs favor a split. A partition gives the west of the country to Europe's trade and weapons, and the eastern approaches to Russian depth, with a line both sides can fortify. The line is what remains when the one thing they cannot share stays unshared.

The model prints two borders, and it ranks them by what they cost. The default result is a freeze along the fortified front in the east and the south. Crimea stays on the Russian side of the books because Sevastopol is the Black Sea Fleet's harbor, and a hostile coast there would put the fleet under an enemy shore. The land link to that harbor, and the Donbas belt in front of it, work in Russian planning as depth. West of the freeze, the remaining state keeps Kyiv, the cities on the central river, and the Polish frontier. It integrates with European markets and keeps receiving weapons. Full membership in the alliance stays blocked, because membership would force the alliance to treat a new war as its own war. The great power's red line is kept by taking the contested east out of the membership question, at the price of a smaller Ukraine.

The second border is the river. The Dnieper is the strongest natural barrier on this plain. A line there would give Russia depth from the eastern regions to a bank it can defend, and it would give the western state a shorter frontier. The model does not pick this line as the normal result. Reaching it takes a second, larger war after a land link to Crimea already exists, and each extra kilometer buys less safety than it costs. Move the border only while the next kilometer's safety is worth more than its cost. The curves cross, in the basic case, on the present front. They cross on the river only if Moscow decides that any Western-armed force east of the water can still threaten the land bridge and the fleet, and if it is willing to pay for that judgment.

Either result has to deal with the capital. A line that cuts through a capital is not a border. It is a crisis that never ends. The contact-line state leaves the capital in the western half, far enough from the freeze to govern, close enough that the loss stays politically alive. That political life is why the western half will not sign a cheerful recognition. Mearsheimer expects a split on the ground, and a protest that never stops. Sachs still argues one neutral country would have cost less than either line. Both can accept the same fact. Once blood has changed the price, neither side will trade at the prewar rate. The old plan remains readable. It is no longer the result that holds.

The freeze lasts only if the same choice comes around again. Each side can cheat. The punishment for cheating is a return to war, which is expensive, so the line holds when a quick gain from a surprise attack is smaller than the long cost of starting the war again. You cannot look inside intentions, so the game cannot rest on promises. It rests on limits that still work if the other side is hostile: an empty strip, limits on long-range weapons parked against the line, and monitors whose budgets do not grow if the line moves. Rebuilding armies so that a seasonal attack looks cheap will break the freeze. The partition is final only while cheating stays expensive.

Money locks the two halves to different patrons. That is Jiang's quiet mechanism. The western state's rebuilding is priced in euros and dollars, watched by European institutions and the International Monetary Fund, and allowed only if the security policy suits those lenders. Conditionality is membership by another name. The eastern zone's fuel, pensions, and trade clear through Russian budgets and payment systems. Over time the two zones stop fitting as one economy, even if a diplomat still shows one map. Grain moving out of the Black Sea is the side payment that keeps the game quiet. A corridor that lets farming earn foreign money gives both sides a cash reason to prefer the freeze to the gun. Close the corridor, and that side payment disappears. The game returns to nothing but security.

Sachs would still call this a failure of institutions, not a law of nature. One neutral state with shared inspectors was a plan for several centers of power. A split is what follows when one leading power meets a great power willing to spend heavily to stop a hostile army on its border. The Military-Industrial Complex only has to keep supplying the forward bases that made neutrality look like giving up. Mearsheimer's reply is that a plan exists only if the strongest players will give up the alignment. They were not willing. The buffer was then filled with weapons, and weapons are facts.

The map that comes out is spare. Blue alliance territory stands on the Polish, Slovak, Hungarian, and Romanian borders, a wall rather than a cushion. Amber and gray mark the smaller Ukrainian state, armed from outside, paid from the west, and not reconciled. A darker band marks the eastern and southern lands under Russian force, including the fleet harbor, tied to another payment system. Between them sits a thin watched strip, the last of the width. The Dnieper stays in the background as the line a bigger war would reach, drawn dashed because the basic case does not pay for it. The caption says the result holds when a promise cannot be enforced, that it costs more than the other plans, and that the center could not be shared.
