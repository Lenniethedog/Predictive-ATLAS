Germany's postwar bargain had two prices. Security was priced in Washington. Energy was priced in Siberia. The second price has been deleted. Pipeline gas from Russia is offline. In the state's own arithmetic the deletion is a cost shock. When the energy term leaves the equation, the security term stops being a gift and becomes an industrial tax. Berlin's rational question is how much tax the industrial core can pay before the core ceases to be a core.

Before the break, more than half of Germany's imported gas came from Russia. The molecule was cheap because it moved by pipe, without liquefaction, without a long ocean leg, and without a regasification toll. It fed boilers, and it fed chemistry. In German industry gas is a feedstock as well as a fuel. A nitrogen plant and a specialty-chemical line do not experience a price spike as a household experiences a bill. They experience it as a competitor in the United States or in China who still buys energy at a lower structural cost. The pipelines that carried the Siberian gas were destroyed in twenty twenty-two. Who destroyed them remains contested. The economic fact is not contested. The gas is not flowing, and nothing in current allied policy intends to turn it back on.

John Mearsheimer looks at this shock through the ranking of powers. Germany is the state most capable of anchoring an independent European pole, which is why other powers have spent a century preventing a durable German-Russian combination and why Washington has an interest in German dependence on American security. Dependence is useful under anarchy because intentions are opaque. A Germany that can fuel itself from the east and sell machines in every direction is a Germany that can disagree. A Germany that must buy expensive allied energy, host allied forces, and clear its export policy with a sanctions regime is a Germany whose relative power is capped. The cap starts to retire factories. Survival for a trading state is the preservation of its productive base. A security umbrella that arrives bundled with deindustrialization fails that definition even if the umbrella is genuine.

Jeffrey Sachs' institutional reading names the same shock as a choice. Eurasian energy interdependence was a stabilizer that the unipolar project reclassified as a vice. Sanctions, the severing of pipeline projects, and the demand that Europe reorient its molecules toward seaborne allied gas were strategic decisions centered in Washington's hierarchy of threats. The costs fell on German chemical workers, on suppliers in the eurozone, and on household bills. The gains showed up in American liquefied-gas exports and in a Military-Industrial Complex that received a larger European mission the moment Europe felt physically exposed. A system that equates allied autonomy with allied risk will keep producing policies that trade European industry for American strategic comfort. A multipolar institution would have treated Russian gas as a negotiated dependence, with storage, diversification, and contracts.

The substitution on offer is real, and it is structurally dearer. Seaborne liquefied gas must be cooled, shipped, and warmed again. Terminals on the German coast, built at emergency speed, solved the physical absence of the molecule. They did not solve the price regime. Long-term purchases from the United States and from other exporters also carry a political premium. A cargo that arrives from an ally can be delayed by an ally's domestic politics, redirected to a higher bidder, or tied to a wider diplomatic ask. Energy security purchased in that market is security with a veto attached. Professor Jiang files the veto under weaponized interdependence. The same state that provides the security umbrella can influence the energy invoice. The customer who has no second system pays for both.

Place Berlin's choice in three terms. The first term is the unit cost of energy relative to the United States and to China, the two rivals for the next factory. The second term is export revenue, especially machinery and vehicles sold into the Chinese market, which remains among Germany's largest external demand pools. The third term is the insurance value of American protection, including the nuclear questions Germany has chosen not to answer alone. A quiet pivot is a reweighting of these terms. Formal membership in the alliance can stay. The change is in the constraints Germany will accept on the first two terms in order to keep the third.

Watch how the reweighting shows up in decisions that look technical. German diplomats resist the widest version of secondary sanctions, the version that would block ordinary industrial sales to China. German executives delay the write-off of chemical capacity and ask which molecule could return quietly. The finance ministry discovers that a shrunken industrial tax base makes the defense spending pledged under alliance pressure harder to fund. Jiang would call this a feedback loop. The security tax reduces the industrial surplus that was supposed to pay the security tax. Past a threshold, loyalty and capacity move in opposite directions. The state then chooses capacity.

The threshold has a date only in the model. Call it the quarter when the present value of lost chemical, glass, metal, and machine-tool capacity exceeds the present value of the protection embedded in the American umbrella. No one announces the crossing. It appears as a change in the default answer to a licensing question, as a long-term gas contract written with a non-allied supplier through a broker, as a refusal to fund a sanctions package that would hit the auto export lane, as a quiet argument inside the eurozone that industrial survival is a European interest. Mearsheimer would recognize the pattern. States do not advertise the moment they begin to hedge a patron. The hedge is assembled from exceptions.

The eurozone is the multiplier. German intermediates sit inside Italian, French, and Central European products. A hollowed German core becomes a fiscal event for the currency union. France, with a nuclear-heavy power system, does not feel the gas deletion in the same organs, and will not lead this pivot. The Commission has a different objective function, closer to rule consistency and to the American file. Berlin therefore moves more slowly in Brussels than in its own licensing offices, and the gap between the European speech and the national permission is the width of the pivot. Currency follows the same gap. Trade that can be invoiced in euros, or netted on a bilateral basis with non-dollar partners, reduces the share of German commerce that a dollar sanction can reach.

Russia's role in the endgame should be stated without romance. The Siberian molecule remains, in physical terms, the lowest-cost large supply that can reach Central Europe by pipe, if the pipes exist and if politics allow. Germany's model does not require loving the supplier. It requires a price and a reliability number. A renewed flow, even a partial one, even through a route that is not the destroyed Baltic lines, changes the unit-cost term immediately. It also carries the risk that Moscow will use the valve later. Offensive realism says that risk is permanent, because intentions will stay unknown. The institutional answer is the one Germany abandoned under pressure: storage mandates, a diverse contract book, and a refusal to let any single supplier, eastern or western, become a veto. The realist answer now is uglier. Accept a managed dependence on the east for the marginal molecule, while keeping allied gas as the base load, and treat both suppliers as powers rather than as friends.

China is the other hinge, and it is an export hinge. A Germany that loses the Chinese market while paying an American energy premium loses twice. A Germany that keeps the Chinese market must disappoint Washington on the margin of technology and investment controls. The disappointment will be explained as precision. Controls aimed at genuine military technology stay. Controls aimed at the broad industrial relationship are sanded down, delayed, and interpreted narrowly. This is how a trading state conducts a security argument it believes it cannot win in public. Sachs would describe the American pressure as the overreach of a hub that wants its allies' commerce to match its threat picture. Mearsheimer would describe the German resistance as relative-power maintenance. Both descriptions can be true in the same quarter.

The day the eurozone chooses industrial survival is therefore a German day wearing a European coat. The coat is the claim that a continent without a chemical and machine base is not defensible anyway, because defense goods are industrial goods. The body under the coat is a set of exceptions that reopen a Eurasian commercial lane: energy where it can be bought without a speech, exports where they can be licensed without a fight, payments where they can avoid a dollar gate. The alliance remains. The umbrella remains. The monopoly of the umbrella over German grand strategy does not. Under anarchy, that is what a pivot looks like when the pivoting state is still too exposed to slam a door, and too industrial to keep paying a tax that retires its factories.
