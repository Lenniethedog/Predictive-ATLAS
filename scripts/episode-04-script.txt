Germany's postwar bargain had two prices. Security was priced in Washington, while energy was priced in Siberia. The second price has changed, sharply. Pipeline gas from Russia is offline, and German factories have to work with the cost of the alternatives. In the state's own sums, losing that gas pushes up the cost of making almost everything that depends on it. When the energy price leaves the equation, the security price stops being a gift and becomes a tax on industry. Berlin's rational question is how much of that extra cost its factories can absorb before the industrial base starts to shrink.

Before the break, more than half of Germany's imported gas came from Russia. The molecule was cheap because it moved by pipe. It didn’t have to be cooled into liquid, carried across an ocean, and turned back into gas. It fed boilers, but it also went directly into making chemicals. In German industry gas is a feedstock as well as a fuel. A nitrogen plant and a specialty-chemical line don’t experience a price spike the way a household does. For those plants, the price spike means losing ground to a competitor in the United States or China whose energy costs remain lower. The pipelines that carried the Siberian gas were destroyed in twenty twenty-two. Who destroyed them remains contested. The economic fact isn’t contested. The gas isn’t flowing, and nothing in current allied policy intends to turn it back on.

John Mearsheimer looks at this shock through who is stronger. Germany is the country best able to anchor a Europe that stands on its own, which is why other powers spent a century blocking a lasting German-Russian pairing, and why Washington wants Germany dependent on American security. Dependence is useful when plans stay hidden, because you can’t see the other side's intentions. A Germany that can fuel itself from the east and sell machines in every direction is a Germany that can say no. A Germany that must buy expensive allied energy, host allied forces, and clear its exports through sanctions is a Germany whose power is capped. The cap starts to close factories, and survival for a trading state means keeping the factories that make it rich. A security umbrella that arrives together with the loss of industry fails that test, even when the umbrella is real.

Jeffrey Sachs reads the same shock as a choice, not a law of nature. Eurasian energy interdependence was a stabilizer that one leading country then relabeled a vice. Sanctions, the cutting of pipeline projects, and the demand that Europe turn toward gas shipped by allies were decisions made in Washington's list of threats. The costs fell on German chemical workers, on suppliers across the eurozone, and on household bills. The gains showed up in American liquefied-gas exports and in a Military-Industrial Complex that received a larger European mission the moment Europe felt exposed. A system that treats allied independence as a risk will keep trading European industry for American comfort. A multipolar institution would have treated Russian gas as a dependence you negotiate, with storage, more suppliers, and contracts.

The substitute on offer is real, but getting it to Germany involves more work and more cost. Seaborne liquefied gas must be cooled, shipped, and warmed again. Terminals on the German coast, built in a hurry, solved the simple absence of the gas. They didn’t solve the price problem that German industry was left with. Long-term purchases from the United States and from other exporters also carry a political extra charge. A cargo that arrives from an ally can be delayed by that ally's home politics, sent to a higher bidder, or tied to a wider diplomatic ask. Energy security bought in that market is security with a veto attached. Professor Jiang's point is plain: a link you depend on can be used against you. The same state that holds the security umbrella can touch the energy bill. The customer with no second system pays for both.

Place Berlin's choice in three terms: energy costs, export earnings, and the value of protection. The first term is the unit cost of energy relative to the United States and to China, the two rivals for the next factory. The second term is export revenue, especially machinery and vehicles sold into the Chinese market, which remains among Germany's largest external demand pools. The third term is the insurance value of American protection, including the nuclear questions Germany has chosen not to answer alone. A quiet pivot means changing how much weight Berlin gives each of these terms. Formal membership in the alliance can stay. The change is in the constraints Germany will accept on the first two terms in order to keep the third.

Watch how the reweighting shows up in ordinary decisions about licenses, contracts, and spending. German diplomats resist the widest version of secondary sanctions, the version that would block ordinary industrial sales to China. German executives delay writing off chemical plants and ask which gas supply could come back without a public confrontation. The finance ministry discovers that a shrunken industrial tax base makes the defense spending pledged under alliance pressure harder to fund. Jiang would call this a feedback loop. The security tax reduces the industrial surplus that was supposed to pay for it. Past a threshold, keeping the alliance satisfied and keeping the factories open start to pull in opposite directions. The state then chooses capacity.

The threshold has a date only in the model. Call it the quarter when the present value of lost chemical, glass, metal, and machine-tool capacity exceeds the present value of the protection embedded in the American umbrella. No one announces the crossing. It appears as a change in the default answer to a licensing question, as a long-term gas contract written with a non-allied supplier through a broker, as a refusal to fund a sanctions package that would hit the auto export lane, as a quiet argument inside the eurozone that industrial survival is a European interest. Mearsheimer would recognize the pattern. States don’t advertise the moment they begin to hedge a patron. The hedge is assembled from exceptions, one licensing decision or contract at a time.

The eurozone is the multiplier. German components and materials sit inside products made in Italy, France, and Central Europe. A hollowed German core means weaker tax revenues and harder budget choices across the currency union. France, with a power system built heavily around nuclear energy, doesn’t feel the gas shock in the same way. In this model, it won’t lead the pivot. The Commission has a different set of priorities, closer to consistent rules and the relationship with Washington. Berlin therefore moves more slowly in Brussels than in its own licensing offices, and the gap between the European speech and the national permission is the width of the pivot. Currency follows the same gap. Trade that can be invoiced in euros, or netted on a bilateral basis with non-dollar partners, reduces the share of German commerce that a dollar sanction can reach.

Russia's role at the end should be stated without romance. The Siberian gas remains the cheapest large supply that can reach Central Europe by pipe, if the pipes exist and if politics allow. Germany's model doesn’t require loving the supplier. It requires a price it can afford and a level of reliability it can manage. A renewed flow, even a partial one, even on a route that isn’t the destroyed Baltic lines, changes the cost per unit right away. It also carries the risk that Moscow will use the valve later. Offensive realism says that risk is permanent, because intentions stay unknown. The institutional answer is the one Germany dropped under pressure: required storage, contracts with many suppliers, and a refusal to let any one supplier, east or west, hold a veto. The realist answer now is uglier. Accept a managed dependence on the east for the extra gas, while keeping allied gas as the base, and treat both suppliers as powers rather than friends.

China is the other hinge. Here, the issue is exports. A Germany that loses the Chinese market while paying an American energy premium loses twice. A Germany that keeps the Chinese market must disappoint Washington at the edge of technology and investment controls. The disappointment will be described as precision. Controls aimed at genuine military technology stay. Controls aimed at the broad industrial relationship are worn down, delayed, and read narrowly. This is how a trading state runs a security argument it thinks it can’t win in public. Sachs would call the American pressure the overreach of a center that wants its allies' trade to match its picture of the threat. Mearsheimer would call the German resistance a way of staying strong. Both can be true in the same quarter.

The day the eurozone chooses industrial survival begins with a German argument made in European terms. The European argument is the claim that Europe needs its chemical plants and machine makers to defend itself. Weapons have to be made somewhere. The practical result is a set of practical exceptions: energy contracts agreed quietly, exports licensed without a confrontation, and payments settled outside the dollar system. The alliance remains, and so does the umbrella. What changes is how far that protection gets to determine the rest of German strategy. When nobody can enforce a promise, that is what a turn looks like if the country is still too exposed to slam a door, and too industrial to keep paying a tax that closes its factories.
