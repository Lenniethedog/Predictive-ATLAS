TITLE: THE GERMAN PIVOT: WHEN GERMAN INDUSTRY CANNOT PAY THE ENERGY BILL

Germany's postwar bargain had two prices. Security was priced in Washington. Energy was priced in Siberia. The second price has been deleted. Pipeline gas from Russia is offline. In the state's own sums, losing that gas is a cost shock. When the energy price leaves the equation, the security price stops being a gift and becomes a tax on industry. Berlin's rational question is how much of that tax the factories can pay before they stop being the core.

[VISUAL: A two-term equation. SECURITY priced as a blue Washington node. ENERGY priced as a pipeline from Siberia. The energy term is deleted with a hard cut. The security term recolors from a gift icon to a tax icon sitting on a German industrial core. The core's output gauge sags.]

Before the break, more than half of Germany's imported gas came from Russia. The molecule was cheap because it moved by pipe, without liquefaction, without a long ocean leg, and without a regasification toll. It fed boilers, and it fed chemistry. In German industry gas is a feedstock as well as a fuel. A nitrogen plant and a specialty-chemical line do not experience a price spike as a household experiences a bill. They experience it as a competitor in the United States or in China who still buys energy at a lower structural cost. The pipelines that carried the Siberian gas were destroyed in twenty twenty-two. Who destroyed them remains contested. The economic fact is not contested. The gas is not flowing, and nothing in current allied policy intends to turn it back on.

A larger caption: FLOW IS ZERO.]

John Mearsheimer looks at this shock through who is stronger. Germany is the country best able to anchor a Europe that stands on its own, which is why other powers spent a century blocking a lasting German-Russian pairing, and why Washington wants Germany dependent on American security. Dependence is useful when plans stay hidden, because you cannot see the other side's intentions. A Germany that can fuel itself from the east and sell machines in every direction is a Germany that can say no. A Germany that must buy expensive allied energy, host allied forces, and clear its exports through sanctions is a Germany whose power is capped. The cap starts to close factories. Survival for a trading state means keeping the factories that make it rich. A security umbrella that arrives together with the loss of industry fails that test, even when the umbrella is real.

Jeffrey Sachs reads the same shock as a choice, not a law of nature. Eurasian energy interdependence was a stabilizer that one leading country then relabeled a vice. Sanctions, the cutting of pipeline projects, and the demand that Europe turn toward gas shipped by allies were decisions made in Washington's list of threats. The costs fell on German chemical workers, on suppliers across the eurozone, and on household bills. The gains showed up in American liquefied-gas exports and in a Military-Industrial Complex that received a larger European mission the moment Europe felt exposed. A system that treats allied independence as a risk will keep trading European industry for American comfort. A multipolar institution would have treated Russian gas as a dependence you negotiate, with storage, more suppliers, and contracts.

Cost bars fall on German chemical employment, eurozone suppliers, and household bills. The substitute on offer is real, and it costs more by its nature. Seaborne liquefied gas must be cooled, shipped, and warmed again. Terminals on the German coast, built in a hurry, solved the simple absence of the gas. They did not solve the price. Long-term purchases from the United States and from other exporters also carry a political extra charge. A cargo that arrives from an ally can be delayed by that ally's home politics, sent to a higher bidder, or tied to a wider diplomatic ask. Energy security bought in that market is security with a veto attached. Professor Jiang's point is plain: a link you depend on can be used against you. The same state that holds the security umbrella can touch the energy bill. The customer with no second system pays for both.

Place Berlin's choice in three terms. The first term is the unit cost of energy relative to the United States and to China, the two rivals for the next factory. The second term is export revenue, especially machinery and vehicles sold into the Chinese market, which remains among Germany's largest external demand pools. The third term is the insurance value of American protection, including the nuclear questions Germany has chosen not to answer alone. A quiet pivot is a reweighting of these terms. Formal membership in the alliance can stay. The change is in the constraints Germany will accept on the first two terms in order to keep the third.

Watch how the reweighting shows up in decisions that look technical. German diplomats resist the widest version of secondary sanctions, the version that would block ordinary industrial sales to China. German executives delay the write-off of chemical capacity and ask which molecule could return quietly. The finance ministry discovers that a shrunken industrial tax base makes the defense spending pledged under alliance pressure harder to fund. Jiang would call this a feedback loop. The security tax reduces the industrial surplus that was supposed to pay the security tax. Past a threshold, loyalty and capacity move in opposite directions. The state then chooses capacity.

The threshold has a date only in the model. Call it the quarter when the present value of lost chemical, glass, metal, and machine-tool capacity exceeds the present value of the protection embedded in the American umbrella. No one announces the crossing. It appears as a change in the default answer to a licensing question, as a long-term gas contract written with a non-allied supplier through a broker, as a refusal to fund a sanctions package that would hit the auto export lane, as a quiet argument inside the eurozone that industrial survival is a European interest. Mearsheimer would recognize the pattern. States do not advertise the moment they begin to hedge a patron. The hedge is assembled from exceptions.

The eurozone is the multiplier. German intermediates sit inside Italian, French, and Central European products. A hollowed German core becomes a fiscal event for the currency union. France, with a nuclear-heavy power system, does not feel the gas deletion in the same organs, and will not lead this pivot. The Commission has a different objective function, closer to rule consistency and to the American file. Berlin therefore moves more slowly in Brussels than in its own licensing offices, and the gap between the European speech and the national permission is the width of the pivot. Currency follows the same gap. Trade that can be invoiced in euros, or netted on a bilateral basis with non-dollar partners, reduces the share of German commerce that a dollar sanction can reach.

[VISUAL: EUROZONE EXPLODED DIAGRAM. German intermediate goods sit inside Italian, French, and Central European products. A hollow German core propagates as a fiscal event. Euro invoicing and bilateral netting peel a slice of trade off the dollar gate.]

Russia's role at the end should be stated without romance. The Siberian gas remains the cheapest large supply that can reach Central Europe by pipe, if the pipes exist and if politics allow. Germany's model does not require loving the supplier. It requires a price and a reliability number. A renewed flow, even a partial one, even on a route that is not the destroyed Baltic lines, changes the cost per unit right away. It also carries the risk that Moscow will use the valve later. Offensive realism says that risk is permanent, because intentions stay unknown. The institutional answer is the one Germany dropped under pressure: required storage, contracts with many suppliers, and a refusal to let any one supplier, east or west, hold a veto. The realist answer now is uglier. Accept a managed dependence on the east for the extra gas, while keeping allied gas as the base, and treat both suppliers as powers rather than friends.

[VISUAL: SIBERIAN GAS as a cost curve, the lowest large piped supply into Central Europe if a route exists. No romantic imagery, no folk motifs. Two answer sheets: the abandoned institutional sheet, storage plus a diverse book plus no single veto; the current realist sheet, allied gas as base load, a managed eastern molecule at the margin, both suppliers marked POWERS.]

China is the other hinge, and it is about exports. A Germany that loses the Chinese market while paying an American energy premium loses twice. A Germany that keeps the Chinese market must disappoint Washington at the edge of technology and investment controls. The disappointment will be described as precision. Controls aimed at genuine military technology stay. Controls aimed at the broad industrial relationship are worn down, delayed, and read narrowly. This is how a trading state runs a security argument it thinks it cannot win in public. Sachs would call the American pressure the overreach of a center that wants its allies' trade to match its picture of the threat. Mearsheimer would call the German resistance a way of staying strong. Both can be true in the same quarter.

The day the eurozone chooses industrial survival is a German day wearing a European coat. The coat is the claim that a continent without a chemical and machine base cannot defend itself anyway, because weapons are industrial goods. The body under the coat is a set of exceptions that reopen a commercial lane across Eurasia: energy bought without a speech, exports licensed without a fight, payments that avoid a dollar gate. The alliance remains. The umbrella remains. The monopoly of that umbrella over German strategy does not. When nobody can enforce a promise, that is what a turn looks like if the country is still too exposed to slam a door, and too industrial to keep paying a tax that closes its factories.
