A working advanced chip factory in the other side's hands can be, in this model, worse for either capital than a factory that has stopped. It sounds strange until you ask what each side is most afraid of losing. That single ranking builds the semiconductor fortress. Taiwan's leading-edge plants are a prize only in peacetime, when both sides can still draw on them under rules the leader sets. In a war where control might change, the same plants become something each side would rather shut down than hand over. Denial is what wins once the threat of a fight no longer holds people back.

The concentration is what makes the argument matter. One firm on one island makes the dominant share of the world's most advanced logic chips. These are the processors that advanced computing and modern weapons both depend on. The tools that print those chips come from a narrow chain: Dutch lithography machines, American equipment for depositing and etching material, and Japanese materials and optics. A frontier plant is therefore a global machine that happens to sit on land both sides claim. Whoever sets the rules of access to that machine has a say in what the economies depending on it can produce in the future.

John Mearsheimer puts Taiwan where three problems meet, and he won’t separate them. The first is a security promise by a sea power to an island off a rising land power. The second is a technology monopoly that feeds both armies and economies. The third is a shift in power, where the rising state can’t know the leader's intentions, and the leader can’t know the rising state's. Capabilities are what get planned against. The capability in the plants is straightforward: they can make more of the chips needed to train and run advanced systems. Relative power, in this view, includes who has more computing power. A state that expects a long rivalry treats a lasting gap in computing as a threat to its position.

Jeffrey Sachs reads the fortress as a policy, not as a law. Export controls on advanced chips and on the tools that make them, subsidies to move a slice of production onto allied soil, and talk of a technology ceiling for China are the tools of a manager trying to freeze who is on top. The Military-Industrial Complex and the control offices both have reasons to keep the restrictions in place. The cost, on this reading, comes from the lesson Beijing draws about its future inside the system. If the hierarchy is frozen by decree, staying inside the present order means falling further behind. A risky attempt to change Taiwan's political status can then be argued as the less risky path. Sachs' alternative is a deal among several centers: commercial access, with checked limits on military use. A system that offers the rising state only a managed decline should expect that state to treat wreckage as an option it might pay for.

Professor Jiang sets this as a game with four outcomes, and the fortress ranks them. Outcome one is today's limited setup. The island keeps making chips under American control of the tools, and China receives only what the rules allow. This outcome favors Washington only while Beijing accepts where the rules leave it over time. Outcome two is capture that still works. Beijing controls the plants, the workforce stays, yields recover, and computing power shifts hard. For Washington this is the worst outcome in the model. Outcome three is denial. The plants no longer run at the leading edge, whoever holds them. Both sides lose the Taiwan flow. The relative loser is whichever side has fewer workable alternatives when the island stops producing. Outcome four is a takeover that can’t run the plants. The flag changes, but the plants don’t recover their ability to make usable chips.

The substitutes decide who can afford denial. New fabs in the American southwest, in Japan, and in Europe, plus whatever leading-edge capacity sits in Korea, can take a fraction of future demand after a delay of years. They don’t replace the island's volume or pace inside a crisis. On the Chinese side the substitutes are domestic foundries running behind the frontier, using older techniques to approximate newer chips, plus whatever stockpile of imported parts was built before the rules tightened. Stockpiles eventually run out, and older manufacturing processes can’t reproduce the leading edge. In denial, the side with the higher substitute base loses less relative power. Washington can therefore contemplate denial more calmly than capture, and Beijing's best cell is a living workforce.

Once a fight looks likely, the fortress forces a calculation: what are the plants actually worth if you can’t keep them running? If the probability of operating the fabs after a violent change of control is low, then a strategy of grabbing them is a strategy of buying denial at a higher price. The machines need constant maintenance. The tools at the frontier require a foreign supply chain that a war will sever. The people who know how to run the process can leave. The machines can’t replace that experience on their own. Cleanrooms are a promise about air and vibration, and war breaks that promise without a special plan. Nationalization then yields Outcome four unless staff, spare parts, power, and water remain available for a long time. A planner who still sees the other side's success as the worst outcome will prefer that nobody gets a working plant.

Mearsheimer needs each capital to rank the rival's exclusive control as more dangerous than a loss they share. Hidden intentions do the rest. If you can’t be sure the other side will leave the plants alone, you give their capture a real chance, and you prepare the menu that makes capture worthless. Sachs would call those preparations a product of pride. A leader that tries to lock a technology monopoly by tightening controls every year teaches the other player that waiting loses money. The institutional bargain, access with checks on military use, exists to give both players a reason to stay inside Outcome one. Collapse the bargain, and the game slides toward the squares where the plants die.

The peacetime race is therefore a race to make denial survivable. Onshore fabs and domestic Chinese tools each reduce the relative pain of a stoppage. Neither project has to match the island to matter. It just has to change which outcome looks least costly. When Washington believes its substitutes cover the military minimum, denial becomes a tolerable emergency rather than a national wound. When Beijing reaches the same belief, denial deters less and capture is worth less. The present is the dangerous middle: the prize is still unique, and the substitutes aren’t ready.

The strategic menu has three broad choices. Each is a decision about the outcome a state wants, rather than the mechanics of a plant. One option is to keep the status quo by making a fight look unpayable, which is deterrence aimed at Outcome one. A second option, available mainly to Beijing in the model, is to seek control and attempt to run what is captured, knowing that the attempt fails if the people and the tool chain are gone. A third option, available to both, is to treat a working transfer as unacceptable and to plan for a world in which the island's frontier output is interrupted for years. Evacuation of specialized labor, legal blocks on servicing the machines, and the ordinary fragility of the plants under fire all sit in that third option as categories of state choice. They aren’t a sequence of instructions. They’re the reasons Outcome three is plausible even to planners who would profit from Outcome one forever.

Demand for artificial intelligence tightens the screws. As more money chases the same leading-edge wafers, the peacetime prize grows, and a growing prize raises both the urge to capture and the urge to deny. States experience it as a narrowing window in which today's setup still wins. Mearsheimer's prediction is that the window is about power, not about business, and that a rising state and a leading state won’t leave it to company boards. Sachs' prediction is that the controls meant to hold the window open are what convince the rising state the window is a cage. Jiang's prediction is a number. When capture, cut by the low chance of a working plant, is worth less than denial, both sides will act, in a crisis, to make the plant unusable rather than let the other side try.

The fortress, then, isn’t a wall around Taiwan. It’s the way the two capitals compare their choices. It’s an order of preferences around a factory: keep it running, control it, or accept that it stops. Blue supply-chain nodes in the Netherlands, Japan, Korea, and the United States remain the masters of the tools, and substitutes that aren’t yet enough. The amber domestic effort on the mainland remains the second source, still behind. The island sits between them, immensely productive, and safe only while both capitals still prefer a living plant to a dead one. The day the threat stops working, that preference flips for both at once. The rational move then stops being possession. It becomes prevention, because stopping the rival looks safer than keeping the prize. The most advanced chips on earth are, in that hour, less dangerous to each capital as rubble in a clean room than as output in the rival's arsenal, and both capitals can still do that sum without raising their voices.
