An intact advanced chip factory in the other side's hands is, for both Washington and Beijing, a worse outcome than a factory that has stopped producing. That single ranking builds the semiconductor fortress. Taiwan's leading-edge fabs are a prize only in peacetime, when both ecosystems can still draw on them under rules the leader sets. In a war where control might change, the same fabs become an asset each side would rather deny than deliver. Denial is the value that wins once deterrence fails.

The concentration is the fact underneath the drama. One firm on one island fabricates the dominant share of the world's most advanced logic chips, the processors at the far end of the process curve, the parts on which advanced computing and modern weapons both depend. The tools that print those chips come from a narrow chain: Dutch lithography at the extreme end, American equipment for deposition and etch, Japanese materials and optics. A frontier fab is therefore a global machine that happens to sit on contested political real estate. Whoever sets the rules of access to that machine sets a share of the future capital stock of every rich economy.

John Mearsheimer locates Taiwan at the intersection of three problems offensive realism cannot separate. The first is a security commitment by a sea power to an island off a rising continental power. The second is a technology monopoly that feeds military and economic capability. The third is a power transition in which the rising state cannot know the leader's intentions, and the leader cannot know the rising state's. Capabilities are what get planned against. The capability embedded in the fabs is the ability to outproduce the other side in the chips that train and run advanced systems. Relative power, in this frame, includes relative compute. A state that expects a long rivalry treats a permanent disadvantage in compute as a threat to its strategic position.

[VISUAL: THREE PROBLEM LAYERS stacked on Taiwan. Layer one: a blue security commitment from a sea power to an island off a continental power. Layer two: a technology monopoly feeding military and economic capability. Layer three: a power transition, intentions shown as sealed envelopes, capabilities shown as open books. Relative compute is added to the relative-power scale. A permanent compute gap is tagged STRATEGIC POSITION.]

Jeffrey Sachs reads the fortress as a policy, not as a law. Export controls on advanced chips and on the tools that make them, subsidies to relocate a slice of production onto allied soil, and the public language of a technological ceiling for China are the instruments of a unipolar manager trying to freeze a hierarchy. The Military-Industrial Complex and the control bureaucracy share a preference for permanent restriction. The cost, on this account, is the lesson Beijing draws. If the hierarchy is frozen by decree, then time inside the present order is time spent falling behind, and a risky attempt to change the political status of Taiwan can be argued as the less risky path. Sachs' alternative is institutional and multipolar: commercial access under verified limits on military end use. A system that offers the rising state only managed decline should expect the rising state to price wreckage as an option.

Professor Jiang sets the interaction as a game with four outcomes, and the fortress is the preference ordering among them. Outcome one is the constrained status quo. The island keeps fabricating under American tool control, and China receives only what the rules allow. This outcome favors Washington only while Beijing finds the long run tolerable. Outcome two is capture with operation. Beijing controls the fabs and the workforce stays, yields recover, and the relative compute position shifts hard. For Washington this is the worst cell on the board. Outcome three is denial. The fabs no longer run at the frontier, whoever holds the buildings. Both sides lose the Taiwanese flow. The relative loser is whichever side has the weaker substitute. Outcome four is nationalization without capability. The flag changes and the yields do not return.

The substitutes decide who can afford denial. New fabs in the American southwest, in Japan, and in Europe, plus whatever leading-edge capacity sits in Korea, can take a fraction of future demand after a delay of years. They do not replace the island's volume or pace inside a crisis. On the Chinese side the substitutes are domestic foundries running behind the frontier, using older techniques to approximate newer chips, plus whatever stockpile of imported parts was built before the rules tightened. Stockpiles expire. Trailing nodes do not reproduce the leading edge. In denial, the side with the higher substitute base loses less relative power. Washington can therefore contemplate denial more calmly than capture, and Beijing's best cell is a living workforce.

Once a fight looks likely, the fortress forces a calculation. If the probability of operating the fabs after a violent change of control is low, then a strategy of grabbing them is a strategy of buying denial at a higher price. The machines are maintenance-dependent. The tools at the frontier require a foreign supply chain that a war will sever. The people who hold the process in their heads can scatter, and a scattered process does not yield wafers. Cleanrooms are a promise about air and vibration, and war breaks that promise without a special plan. Nationalization then yields Outcome four unless staff, spares, power, and water are held for a long time. A planner who still ranks the other side's success as the worst cell will prefer that nobody gets a working plant.

Mearsheimer needs each capital to rank the rival's exclusive control as more dangerous than a shared loss. Unknowable intentions do the rest. If you cannot be sure the other side will leave the fabs alone, you assign a positive probability to their capture, and you prepare the menu that makes capture worthless. Sachs would call that prepared menu a product of hubris. A leader that tries to lock a technological monopoly by tightening controls every year teaches the other player that waiting has a negative return. The institutional bargain, access with verification, exists to keep both players inside Outcome one. Collapse the bargain, and the game slides toward the cells in which the fabs die.

The peacetime race is therefore a race to make denial survivable. Onshore fabs and domestic Chinese tools each reduce the relative pain of a stoppage. Neither project has to match the island to matter. It has to move the ranking of outcomes. When Washington believes its substitutes cover the military minimum, denial becomes a tolerable emergency rather than a national wound. When Beijing reaches the same belief, denial deters less and capture is worth less. The present is the dangerous middle, with a unique prize and incomplete substitutes.

[VISUAL: PEACETIME RACE, two tracks. Onshore fabs and domestic Chinese tools each shave the pain of a stoppage without matching the island. A ranking meter moves. The strategic menu is coarser than an engineering checklist, and the coarseness is the point. One option is to keep the status quo by making a fight look unpayable, which is deterrence aimed at Outcome one. A second option, available mainly to Beijing in the model, is to seek control and attempt to run what is captured, knowing that the attempt fails if the people and the tool chain are gone. A third option, available to both, is to treat a working transfer as unacceptable and to plan for a world in which the island's frontier output is interrupted for years. Evacuation of specialized labor, legal blocks on servicing the machines, and the ordinary fragility of the plants under fire all sit in that third option as categories of state choice. They are not a sequence of instructions. They are the reasons Outcome three is plausible even to planners who would profit from Outcome one forever.

The AI demand curve tightens the screws. As more capital competes for the same leading-edge wafers, the peacetime prize grows, and a growing prize raises both the incentive to capture and the incentive to deny. States experience it as a narrowing window in which the status quo still dominates. Mearsheimer's prediction is that the window is a power fact, not a commercial one, and that a rising state and a leading state will not leave the fact to corporate governance. Sachs' prediction is that the controls meant to hold the window open are what convince the rising state the window is a cage. Jiang's prediction is numerical. When the expected value of capture, adjusted for the low probability of a working plant, falls below the expected value of denial, both sides will act, in a crisis, to make the plant unusable rather than let the other side try.

The fortress, then, is not a wall around Taiwan. It is a preference ordering around a factory. Blue supply-chain nodes in the Netherlands, Japan, Korea, and the United States remain the tool masters and the incomplete substitutes. The amber domestic effort on the mainland remains the trailing second source. The island sits between them, immensely productive, and safe only while both capitals still prefer a living fab to a dead one. The day deterrence fails, that preference flips for both at once. The rational move ceases to be possession. It becomes prevention. The most advanced chips on earth are, in that hour, less dangerous to each capital as rubble in a cleanroom than as output in the rival's arsenal, and both capitals are capable of doing that arithmetic without raising their voices.
