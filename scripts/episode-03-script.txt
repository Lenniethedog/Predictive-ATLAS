Closing the Strait of Malacca is a supply problem before it is a navy story. China can be cut off from the Gulf only as far as its oil still comes through a southern gate, and only while stocks, Pacific deliveries, and pipelines fail to fill the gap. Beijing has spent twenty years and a great deal of money trying to shrink that gap. The question is how much of it can be closed in a crisis.

The strait is a funnel between Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula, narrowing to a few kilometers of usable lane near Singapore. A large share of the oil and dry cargo moving between the Indian Ocean and the Pacific passes that funnel. The classic Chinese statement of the problem, the Malacca dilemma, treated roughly four-fifths of China's oil imports as hostages of this single passage. Pipelines have since lowered the fraction, but they haven’t made the problem go away. Middle Eastern and African crude still reaches Chinese terminals by riding the southern approaches.

John Mearsheimer reads a blockade as the tool of a sea power. The United States doesn’t have to occupy China's interior to hurt Chinese power. It has to threaten the ships that feed Chinese factories. Nobody is in charge of the whole system, and intentions stay unknown. So Beijing plans for what America can do, rather than relying on a promise that it never will. Energy dependence is an advantage for whoever holds the outer sea. Security is one-sided at a narrow strait, because one navy's open lane is the other navy's closed valve. Relative power is getting to decide which of those it is.

[VISUAL: Two navies abstracted as valve hands, not as ship models in combat. Blue outer-sea control on the Indian Ocean side. Crimson industrial demand on the Pacific side. The chokepoint is a literal valve graphic. Open lane and closed valve are the same object, recolored. Caption: SECURITY AT A CHOKEPOINT IS ZERO-SUM.]

Jeffrey Sachs hears a different story in the same ships. A blockade of energy and food trade is what follows when one country runs out of rules and reaches for force. It confirms, inside the targeted country, that the American system isn’t a safe home. The Military-Industrial Complex can keep the posture going, because a crisis at a narrow strait is a fleet job with a budget line. The act meant to punish China is also the act that makes an energy partnership across Eurasia look like common sense.

Professor Jiang asks for the arithmetic before the coalition. Set Chinese crude imports at eleven million barrels a day, and split that flow by the route it takes. Six million barrels a day still depend on a southern strait. The other five million arrive from the Pacific side or by land, and a Malacca-only stop doesn’t touch them. Put a hypothetical stock of eight hundred million barrels behind the system. That's an assumption for this scenario, rather than a published reserve figure. If every import stopped, the dial would read on the order of seventy days. If only the southern six million stopped, the deficit is six million a day and the same dial reads on the order of four months. Rationing starts before the tanks are empty. The dilemma literature divides stocks by total imports and therefore overstates the speed of a narrow closure.

The composition of the surviving five million is the part of the map that matters. Design capacity on the three inland crude lines is public and limited. The Myanmar line from the Bay of Bengal toward Yunnan is about twenty-two million tons a year. The Kazakhstan line is about twenty million. The spur from Russia's eastern pipeline system into Daqing is about thirty million. Together that is a little under one and a half million barrels a day. The Power of Siberia line, at a design near thirty-eight billion cubic meters a year, displaces some seaborne liquefied gas and leaves the crude gap intact. Russian crude loaded on the Pacific, at Kozmino, never enters the Indian Ocean, and cargoes from the Americas can cross the Pacific directly. Including the Pacific turns the siege into a larger war.

Rerouting is the first counter. Cargoes that can’t use Malacca can try the Sunda Strait, the Lombok Strait, or the eastern Indonesian exits. The journey is longer, which pushes up both freight and insurance. Some of this diversion is already normal, because the largest tankers prefer deeper water. A closure that is real in economic terms has to price every southern gate, not only the famous one. Each added gate adds coastline, adds a third country's territorial sea, and adds a chance that the blockade stops being a bilateral squeeze. Mearsheimer would count that chance as the mechanism by which chokepoint wars widen. The offshore power wants a single valve it can close. What it gets is an archipelago, with other routes that also have to be watched.

The second answer is to use less. Diesel for agriculture and for the armed forces is protected, while private road fuel takes the cut. Export-oriented petrochemicals are cut before household heating. Refineries are told to favor the barrel used at home over the product sold abroad. A modern industrial country can take a partial loss of crude for a season if its grids still have coal, nuclear, hydro, and pipeline gas, and if managers will idle the least necessary factory. The industries that halt first are the ones whose exports earn the foreign money China uses to buy the next cargo. An energy squeeze and a dollar squeeze work together. Tankers inside the blue insurance system stop. Cargoes that still move are the ones paid in yuan, gold, or a two-country balance, under insurance written outside American law.

The third counter is to look overland and toward the Arctic. Pipelines at full design replace a slice of the southern deficit, not the deficit itself. Pushing them harder in a crisis depends on Russian surplus production, on Kazakh politics, and on the security of a Burmese corridor that has its own internal wars. The Northern Sea Route along the Russian Arctic is a summer and shoulder-season lane, shortened on the map between northern energy projects and Chinese ports, and constrained by ice class, icebreakers, insurance, and depth of traffic. Its annual volume is a rounding error beside the oil that normally rides Malacca. It can ease the shortage, but it can’t carry anything like the full volume. Gwadar and the Pakistani corridor are a political option with berth limits and an insurgency problem.

Run the gambit forward one quarter. Month one: ships already east of the gates discharge, and stocks draw down on a published schedule. The external price of tanker insurance spikes. Month two: southern crude is partly rerouted, partly replaced by Pacific Russian barrels, and partly lost. Pipeline utilization is forced upward, while export factories on the south coast take the first enforced holidays. Month three: if the Pacific gate is still open, the system is injured and functioning. If the Pacific gate has been closed as well, stocks approach the level where military and agricultural priorities crowd out the rest, and the industrial core contracts. This is the calculation behind China's peacetime overpayment for redundant routes. The option value equals the probability of a siege, multiplied by the output destroyed per month of shortage. These routes look overpriced in peacetime. Include the risk of a siege, and the extra spending starts to look like insurance.

Mearsheimer's warning sits on the third month. A continental power that watches its stocks fall doesn’t experience the blockade as a limited navy measure. It experiences it as an attempt on the political survival of the state. Beijing could respond with economic payback, pressure on other countries, or attacks on the blockading fleet. Such moves rest on one idea: states rank survival above the other side's rules for a fight. Washington's planners know this, which is why a real blockade is tied to a Taiwan crisis rather than switched on as a punishment by itself. The energy valve is an add-on to a larger fight. Each additional move gives that fight another way to spread beyond what its authors intended.

Sachs would count a different loss. Allies who depend on the same strait for their own Gulf oil are asked to bless a closure that raises their prices and their shipping risk. Gulf producers are asked to give up Chinese demand, or to accept America policing their customers. The unipolar habit assumes that those countries will accept the demands. They have costs and interests of their own. A multipolar energy market gives the producers a second buyer and, after recent freezes of reserves, a reason to keep that buyer. Coercion at the strait speeds up the shift from the first episode: the next barrel billed outside the dollar, because the dollar system has shown it can stop the ship.

The plain result is two different estimates, because these are two different wars. China can endure a Malacca-only closure for months, with damage, by drawing stocks, sending ships the long way, pushing pipelines to about one and a half million barrels a day, and leaning on Pacific crude. China can’t endure a full southern-and-Pacific energy siege for long without factories shutting down hard, and that fuller siege is a regional war, not just a strait. Beijing's gambit is to make a narrow closure achieve too little, and a wider closure cost far too much. Washington's gambit is to threaten both, hoping the threat changes Chinese behavior before either option has to be tested. Neither side can be sure what the other will do, because intentions stay hidden. Both prepare for the test anyway.
